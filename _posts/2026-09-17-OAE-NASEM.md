This week the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine published the first installment of its updated research strategy for marine carbon dioxide removal (mCDR). I served on the standing committee that produced it, focusing my work on the [module on ocean alkalinity enhancement](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/pubs/29342/versions/v1.0/modules/module-8.html), or OAE.

The idea behind OAE is simple enough. Add alkalinity to seawater and the carbonate chemistry shifts: dissolved CO<sub>2</sub> converts to bicarbonate and carbonate ions, surface waters can take up more CO<sub>2</sub> from the atmosphere, and the carbon stored that way stays put for centuries to millennia. The geochemistry is well established, essentially an acceleration of the natural rock weathering that has regulated atmospheric CO<sub>2</sub> over geologic time.

The new module is a substantially expanded treatment of OAE compared to the chapter in the [2022 report](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/26278), reflecting how much has happened in four years: in-water field trials, mesocosm and laboratory programs across many institutions, a fast-growing modeling literature, and companies selling carbon credits. Important research is being done and we have learned a great deal.

The central message of our assessment is that OAE remains promising and still uncertain, and these uncertainties get sharper the larger you imagine the deployment. Open ocean field trials, such as [LOC-NESS](https://locness.whoi.edu/) in 2025, are answering important questions, but these are still quite small scale. Removing carbon at a scale that measurably changes atmospheric CO<sub>2</sub> raises others we cannot yet answer. Four stand out to me.

**First, we do not yet understand the processes that follow an intervention well enough to model them.** When alkalinity enters seawater, a chain of physical, chemical and biological processes determines how much CO<sub>2</sub> is actually drawn down. Minerals dissolve at rates that are poorly constrained for most candidate products, and if particles sink before dissolving, they are lost. If the local saturation state gets too high, calcium carbonate precipitates and consumes the alkalinity just added. Air-sea CO<sub>2</sub> exchange is slow: the classic estimate is six months for a surface mixed layer to equilibrate, during which currents carry that water away and may subduct it out of contact with the atmosphere altogether. Uptake is therefore both delayed and spread over a wide area. Models are essential, and they have advanced, but we need much more validation against in-water observations. 

**Second, distinguishing intervention carbon from everything else is hard.** The ocean already absorbs roughly one-third of [anthropogenic CO<sub>2</sub> emissions](https://stories.leap.carbonplan.org/ocean-sink), and this natural sink will remain far larger than any mCDR signal for the foreseeable future. Layered on top is enormous natural variability in space and time. Demonstrating additionality means detecting a small, deliberate perturbation against a large, noisy, shifting background. My own group works on [measuring and modeling this sink](https://oceancarbon.ldeo.columbia.edu), and uncertainties remain large, particularly at the local scale most relevant to mCDR.

**Third, a large deployment must be net negative, and that is not automatic.** Mining, grinding, transporting and dispersing alkaline material all consume energy and emit carbon. Finer grinding speeds dissolution but raises electricity demand; electrochemical approaches need large seawater throughput and low-carbon power. Transparent, consistent life-cycle accounting (LCA) has to be part of any carbon removal claim, and the report finds current practices inconsistent enough to make approaches hard to compare.

**Fourth, the raw resource requirements are enormous.** Gigaton-scale removal by mid-century would require scale-up rates faster than almost any historical technology analogue. Global limestone resources are probably not the binding constraint, but achievable rates of quarrying, kiln capacity, processing, transport and at-sea dispersal may well be. Resources are also unevenly distributed, creating national and regional logistical problems that a global average hides.

None of this is an argument against OAE research. It is an argument for doing that research carefully, at a pace that lets the science keep up with deployment.

There are also significant policy and governance challenges. My Columbia colleague Romany Webb, who served on both the 2022 committee and this one, has written an excellent [summary of the governance findings](https://blogs.law.columbia.edu/climatechange/2026/09/16/new-national-academies-of-sciences-report-on-marine-carbon-dioxide-removal-highlights-the-need-for-governance-reforms/) on the Sabin Center's Climate Law Blog. As she notes, governance gaps directly constrain how quickly OAE could scale: international agreements written for other purposes fit awkwardly around an activity that perturbs the ocean in order to benefit the climate, and most countries have no legal framework tailored to mCDR at all. Her post is well worth reading in full.

We need every viable tool for addressing climate change, and the ocean is too large a part of the carbon cycle to ignore. But the case for OAE has to be built on evidence we do not yet have. That is what the research agenda in this report is for.
